# Comparing Cases, Groups, and Contradictions Within Qualitative Material

Comparing across cases, participant segments, or time points is where qualitative analysis often moves from description to explanation. The question shifts from "what is here" to "how does this pattern hold up when we look elsewhere." A researcher-led AI tool supports this comparative work by making it practical to test emerging interpretations against the full dataset, not just the most vivid examples.

Qualitative datasets are rarely homogeneous. A study of 40 interview participants might include healthcare providers, patients, and administrators. A focus group project might span five cities. Even a single-case longitudinal study contains temporal segments that need comparison. Once a researcher has identified a pattern in one subset, say, frustration with referral processes among patients, checking whether the same pattern appears, differs, or is absent in another subset requires systematic retrieval across the entire corpus. The practical difficulty is that manual approaches handle this through careful coding schemes that tag each segment with case attributes. That works, but it assumes the researcher already knows which attributes matter before the comparison begins. AI-assisted tools that support conversational querying change this: a researcher can ask "what do administrators say about referral delays compared to patients" without having pre-coded every segment for speaker role. The AI retrieves relevant passages from both groups, and the researcher evaluates whether the evidence supports a meaningful difference.

Good qualitative analysis does not only confirm emerging patterns. It actively searches for material that contradicts them. A theme around "staff felt well-supported" becomes more interesting when the researcher can quickly locate the three participants who described the opposite experience and examine whether those cases share something in common that the dominant pattern misses. A tool that returns a summary of what participants said about support levels is less useful than one that returns specific passages from specific participants, with source references the researcher can inspect. The contradiction may be a genuine counter-example that forces revision of the interpretation. It may also be a matter of context: one participant's "support" refers to peer relationships, another's refers to formal training. The researcher needs to see the original material to decide. Negative case analysis also benefits from the iterative, conversational workflow. A researcher might start with a broad question ("where do participants disagree about the new policy?"), review the returned passages, notice that disagreement clusters by department, then refine the question ("what do IT staff say specifically about the implementation timeline?"). Each cycle narrows or widens the view without losing the connection back to source material.

A typical comparative workflow in a researcher-led AI tool might look like this. First, the researcher identifies the segmentation relevant to the analysis: participant role, site location, time period, demographic characteristic. This can be based on metadata already in the project or on emergent groupings the researcher has identified during analysis. Second, rather than running separate analyses for each group, the researcher asks comparative questions: "how do first-time participants describe the onboarding process compared to returning participants?" The AI retrieves passages from both groups, preserving source references for each. Third, the researcher reads the returned material not as a final answer but as a starting point for assessment. Are the passages representative? Does the AI's retrieval miss relevant material from one group? Is the comparison fair, or does it collapse meaningful differences within groups? Fourth, where evidence diverges between groups, the researcher records this explicitly. A tool that supports annotation or memo-writing alongside the retrieved passages helps build the analytical record. The goal is not to eliminate contradictions but to account for them in the interpretation. Fifth, a strong comparative analysis also considers whether the apparent difference is real or an artifact of how the question was framed. The researcher might re-query using different phrasing, or examine whether the same question asked of the full dataset returns a different picture than the group-specific comparison.

Interpretive responsibility means the researcher does not simply report what the AI retrieved. They also document what they considered but set aside. An alternative reading might be: "The apparent consensus on training satisfaction among managers may reflect social desirability in a group setting, given that focus group dynamics among peers in the same organisation can suppress criticism." That interpretive judgment is the researcher's, supported by evidence the AI helped surface but not produced by it. This documentation matters for auditability. A reader of the final analysis should be able to see not only what the researcher concluded, but what counter-evidence was examined and why it did not overturn the interpretation. In practice, this means the tool's output, retrieved passages, query logs, annotations, becomes part of the analytical record, not a disposable intermediate step.

Comparative analysis via AI is not immune to the general limitations of LLM-assisted qualitative work. The AI may converge on dominant patterns within a group and underrepresent minority perspectives within it. It may retrieve plausible-sounding passages that are not actually representative. Context loss is a particular risk in cross-group comparison, because the AI may select passages that match the query terms without preserving the conversational or narrative context that gives them meaning. These are not reasons to avoid comparative queries. They are reasons to treat the AI's output as material for researcher evaluation, not as findings. The traceability of each returned passage to its source is the condition that makes that evaluation possible. Without it, the researcher has no way to assess whether the comparison is accurate or misleading. The comparative workflow is strongest when it remains a dialogue: the researcher asks, the AI returns evidence, the researcher assesses, refines, and asks again. That iterative process, grounded in source material and governed by researcher judgement, is what distinguishes researcher-led analysis from automated comparison that produces conclusions without accountability.
