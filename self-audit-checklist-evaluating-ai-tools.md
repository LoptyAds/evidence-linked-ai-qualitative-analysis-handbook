# Self-Audit Checklist: Evaluating Any AI-Assisted Qualitative Tool for Researcher Judgment

## Scope of This Checklist

This checklist is for researchers evaluating AI-assisted qualitative analysis tools. It is not a feature comparison table. It defines criteria for preserving researcher judgment versus replacing it with automated output.

The criteria apply across tools, methodologies, and paradigms. They stem from one distinction: AI that assists analysis versus AI that performs analysis while the researcher reviews.

---

## Criterion 1: Researcher-Directed Questioning

Does the tool let you ask questions of your material, or does it only offer pre-built outputs?

A tool that generates themes, summaries, or code suggestions is directing the analysis. The researcher responds to what the tool produces. A tool supporting researcher-directed questioning lets you ask specific, comparative, exploratory questions and returns results grounded in the material.

Ask during evaluation:

- Can I ask "What reservations did participants express about this approach?" or "How do perspectives differ between these two groups?"
- Or am I limited to buttons labeled "Generate themes" or "Summarize this segment"?
- Does the tool allow follow-up questions that refine or redirect the inquiry?

Qualitative analysis is iterative and driven by emerging questions. A tool that cannot respond to researcher questions is a report generator, not an analytical partner.

---

## Criterion 2: Source References in Outputs

When the tool produces a claim, can you see what material supports it?

This is not about data storage. It is about whether each analytical output, theme, comparison, pattern, summary, links to the specific source passages supporting it. Without this, you cannot verify, check whether the AI selected representative evidence or cherry-picked, or assess whether context was preserved.

Check:

- Does the tool return supporting source passages alongside each claim?
- Are those passages directly accessible, clickable, quotable, inspectable?
- Or does the tool produce standalone summaries with no way to trace claims back to evidence?

Source references do not make an interpretation correct. They make it inspectable. That is a precondition for researcher judgment, not a substitute.

---

## Criterion 3: AI Proposes, Researcher Decides

Does the AI present findings as suggestions or as conclusions?

Some tools frame output as "themes discovered by AI" or "insights generated automatically." That framing shifts interpretive authority to the model, reducing the researcher to reviewer. Other tools present the same material as proposals: "Based on the selected passages, these patterns appear. You decide whether they hold, need refinement, or miss something."

Test this by examining the tool's first output after you upload data. Is the language definitive or provisional? Can you reject, modify, or ignore what the AI offers without breaking the workflow? Does the tool treat your override as valid or as a deviation from its "correct" analysis?

---

## Criterion 4: Inspectability and Override

Can you see exactly what the AI did, and can you change it?

Inspectability means you can see which passages the AI grouped together, which criteria it used, and which patterns it identified. Override means you can intervene, split a group, reassign a passage, discard a suggestion, add your own observation, and the tool respects that intervention as part of the analytical record.

Some tools treat AI outputs as a first pass you can edit. Others treat them as a finished analysis you can annotate. Those are different. Editing changes the analysis. Annotation adds commentary alongside it. A tool that only supports annotation does not support override.

---

## Criterion 5: Iterative, Non-Linear Workflows

Real qualitative analysis is not a pipeline. You go broad, then narrow. You follow a lead, hit a contradiction, go back to another part of the data, refine your question, and try again.

Does the tool support this? Or does it assume a linear sequence: upload → process → export?

Look for:

- The ability to move between a broad view of the dataset and focused questions on specific segments.
- The ability to compare across cases or groups, then return to individual passages.
- The ability to change your analytical direction without restarting from scratch.
- The ability to preserve the history of your inquiry, what you asked, what you found, what you changed.

A tool that forces a fixed analytical sequence is imposing methodology by design. That may be acceptable for some projects. But it should be a conscious choice, not a hidden constraint.

---

## Applying the Checklist

These criteria are not pass/fail. A tool might score well on researcher-directed questioning but poorly on source references. Another might support override but only within a linear workflow. The point is to know what you are getting.

When evaluating any tool, run it against a small dataset you know well, ideally one you have already analyzed manually. Ask questions you already know the answer to. See whether the tool lets you find what you expect, whether it surfaces things you missed, and whether you can challenge its outputs when they are wrong.

The question is not whether the AI is smart. The question is whether you remain in control of the analysis.
